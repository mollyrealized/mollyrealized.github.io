Approach

The part of the job that does not fit in a bullet point.

Reading for argument

My best work happens when I am fully engaged with what I am reading — not just moving through it, but actually understanding it.

In litigation support that matters more than it might seem. A pleading is making an argument, and if I am following that argument as it builds, I catch things a straight proofread would not. Sometimes it is a position in one section that quietly conflicts with something argued three pages later. Sometimes it is a missing word that inverts the entire meaning of a sentence.

When I find something like that, I do not just flag it. I explain what I found and why it is a problem, so whoever is making the final call actually understands it.

I proofread not just for grammar and spelling, but for context and consistency — I can usually comprehend the subject matter well enough to notice when an attorney meant to insert a not.

That is the difference between checking a document and reading one. A spell-checker will pass a sentence that argues the opposite of what counsel intended. I would rather be the person who catches it before it is filed than the person who explains it afterward.

Precision in writing

I care about clarity in writing, probably more than is strictly necessary. My instinct is to cut what does not need to be there and make sure what is left cannot be misread. That is genuinely satisfying to me — not just as task completion, but as a thing done right.

It shows up in small ways. I know when to use i.e. and when to use e.g. I know that a caption is not a place to improvise. I know which parts of a document a reader will skim and which parts they will hold you to.

Trust and turnaround

My career has consistently been the kind of position where you stay on top of multiple attorneys’ desks while simultaneously being part of a team that handles each other’s overflow. That works on trust, and trust is built the same way every time: good working relationships, keeping people in the loop, and taking personal responsibility for quality and turnaround.

Keeping people in the loop is not a courtesy. It is what lets an attorney make a decision with the actual state of things in front of them.

Concretely, that means an attorney should never learn about a problem from the court. If a filing is going to be tight, if a rule is ambiguous, if I found something in the draft that changes the calculus — that reaches them while there is still time to act on it.

Improving what I touch

Over twenty-five years I have kept building the things that were missing: court-link reference sheets, practical workflow guides, PTO tracking, a contract-tracking database, a firm’s first employment manual. None of it was assigned. It accumulated because a process with friction in it is interesting to me rather than merely irritating.

The same instinct is why I ended up reporting bugs and proposing features directly to NetDocuments developers alongside my firm’s CTO. I notice where a system fights its users, and I would rather describe the problem well enough to be fixed than work around it for another decade.

What I am looking for

A long-term position — a work home, in the sense that Howard & Howard was one for twelve years. Litigation, ideally supporting attorneys with active trial practices who need staff ready and able to be there for the critical moments. That is the work I am best at and the work I want.
